# "Inabilities"

## Overview

"Inabilities" is a poem about a first date narrated from inside the one place the date never reaches: the speaker's head. A man walks into a room toward a woman he barely knows, and in the twenty-nine lines it takes him to cross it, the reader is handed everything she will not be — the rehearsed walk, the scripted conversation, the rationed eye contact, the drug taken on the way over, and the catalogue of things he has already decided to hide. The title sounds like a line from an assessment form, and the poem is in fact an inventory of competence, conducted mid-performance. But the inventory is rigged, because what it actually documents is a staggering set of *abilities* — thirty-seven years of study, a script written the night before, a whole man built on the sidewalk — and the cost of maintaining them. The poem's cruelest discovery arrives in its final stanza: the fear is not that she will hate him. It is that she will love him, and that her love will land on the performance, while the person it was built to protect waits outside, still practicing the smallest word in the language.

## Thematic Analysis

### The Practice

The first stanza is procedural to the point of flatness — door, room, table, bee-line — and it is flat on purpose, because its last two lines detonate everything above them: "I practiced this walk the whole way here / I practice everything." The reader must immediately reread the stanza and find that nothing in it was spontaneous. The "slow pace" was choreography. The bee-line was blocking. Even the confidence of "bee-line" — the one verb in the stanza that sounds instinctive, animal, unwilled — turns out to have been rehearsed on the sidewalk. "I practice everything" is the poem's thesis, and it is five words long because a man who practices everything has learned to keep his sentences short enough to deliver correctly.

The one phrase that escapes the choreography is "her lovely presence." It is the only tenderness in the poem, and the word doing the work is "presence" — because presence is precisely what the speaker does not have. She simply *is* at the table. He has to build his being-there by hand, on the way over, out of rehearsal and chemistry. The gap between her presence and his performance is the poem's whole subject, named in passing in its fourth line.

### The Inventory of Can't

The second stanza runs its anaphora down a deliberately descending scale: "I can't hold up a household / I can't hold down a full-time job / I can't even keep a healthy conversation going." Household, job, conversation — each unit smaller than the last, and "even" is the word that admits the direction of travel. The list is engineered so that the smallest, most ordinary competence is the one that fails, because that is the failure that costs the most on a first date.

Then the poem does something more honest than the list: it qualifies it. "At least not without a script I wrote the night before" concedes that the conversation *is* possible — as authored text. This is the poem's picture of masking, drawn without ever using the word: conversation as a document prepared in advance, "eye contact in three-second doses," social contact titrated like medication. "Doses" is the hinge word of the entire poem. It imports pharmacology into intimacy one stanza before the actual pharmacology arrives, so that by the time the reader learns what was snorted in the car, the vocabulary has already confessed: for this speaker, human contact and chemical management are the same category of act, measured in the same units.

The stanza's last two lines are its verdict. "Thirty-seven years of studying people" — studying, not living among; the word makes him an anthropologist embedded in his own species — "And I still need something in my blood to pass for one." To pass *for* a person: the line treats personhood as a credential that can be forged, and "pass" carries its full double weight — passing a test, and passing as something you have been told you are not. The horror of the line is its arithmetic. Thirty-seven years of fieldwork was not enough; the study had to be supplemented by chemistry; and the sentence records this not with self-pity but with the exhausted precision of a man reporting a lab result.

### The Three Confessions

The third stanza opens with an idiom used as a trap. "My mind goes for the gutter" promises the reader lust — that is what the gutter means — and then delivers inventory instead: "A hundred things that I'll hide from her." The tense matters enormously. Not things I *hide* — things I *will* hide. The concealment is not a state; it is a policy, being ratified in real time, at the table, during the date. The reader is watching the decision get made.

What follows is the poem's structural signature: three bare possessive nouns, each followed by one line of terrible specificity. "My addictions" is an abstraction the reader could slide past; "What I snorted on my way over so my brain would sit still" cannot be slid past, because its timeline collapses into the present — the drug is in him *now*, at this table, in this poem. And "so my brain would sit still" borrows the language of childhood discipline, the thing said to a squirming boy in a classroom, and aims it at his own neurology. The drug is not pleasure here. It is obedience, administered so the brain will behave in public.

"My desires" arrives next, and after "gutter," the reader has been set up to expect the illicit. Instead: "To be loved by someone who's met the actual me." It is the chastest line in the poem, and the most impossible — impossible by construction, because the policy ratified four lines earlier ("a hundred things that I'll hide from her") guarantees that no one at this table will ever meet the actual him. The desire and the strategy cancel each other. The stanza states the poem's central trap without underlining it: the performance built to make love possible makes the only love he wants unreachable.

"My emergencies" completes the triptych, and its expansion is the poem's darkest line: "The mornings I woke up surprised to still be alive." Emergencies, plural — recurring, and evidently never reported, since an emergency that reaches no one keeps its owner's name off every record but this poem. The line refuses to specify whether the surprise follows the needle or the despair, and the refusal is the point: in this speaker's mornings, the overdose and the ideation have stopped being distinguishable categories.

### The Worse Outcome

The final stanza begins with the expected fear and immediately outbids it: "What if she hates me? / Or worse, what if she falls for me?" The inversion is the poem's sharpest thought. Hatred, at least, would land on the mask — the performance absorbs the rejection, and the actual self remains unjudged. But love is the catastrophe, because "What if it's the performance she falls for?" would prove the unbearable thing: that the built man outcompetes the born one. Her love would arrive as evidence — final, intimate, smiling evidence — that the mask works better than the face.

Then the poem splits its speaker in two, grammatically and geographically. "The man I built on the sidewalk" is third person — a construction, minutes old, with a fabrication site named. "The real me waits outside" is first person, and outside — the actual self never entered the room. The poem has been narrated by a double the whole time, and its final image is exile: "High, stimming, unbearable." Three adjectives, one from each of the poem's hidden files — the drug, the diagnosis, the verdict. "Stimming" is the bravest word in the poem, the only moment either unnamed condition surfaces in its own vocabulary, and it appears exactly once, outside, where no one on the date will hear it. "Unbearable" is not the speaker's word; it is the world's word, internalized so thoroughly it now files as self-description.

And then the last line: "Out there still practicing how to say hello." The poem opened with a door successfully walked through and closes with the smallest unit of social language — one word, two syllables — still in rehearsal after thirty-seven years. The performance made it to the table. The person never got past hello.

## Literary Devices

### The Split Screen

The poem's governing device is dramatic irony run as a split screen: the woman at the table receives the performance while the reader receives the inventory. Every line of warmth in the scene (the bee-line, the lovely presence) plays simultaneously against the feed only the reader can hear (the rehearsal, the drug, the policy of concealment). The date and the confession happen in the same lines at the same time, and the woman's entire experience of the evening — presumably pleasant, possibly promising — is constructed out of material the reader knows to be authored. The poem makes its reader the only person who has ever actually met its speaker.

### The Rigged Inventory

The title promises a list of deficits, and the anaphora delivers one — but the poem systematically records the opposite. A man who cannot keep a conversation going writes one the night before. A man who cannot pass for a person has spent thirty-seven years studying how, and builds a working replica on a sidewalk in the minutes before a date. The device is an inventory whose every entry disproves its own heading: these are not inabilities but abilities so costly they have bankrupted their owner. The title is the speaker's accounting error, and the poem lets the reader catch it.

### Borrowed Registers

The poem assembles its confession from four borrowed vocabularies: pharmacology ("doses," "something in my blood," "high"), theater ("practice," "script," "performance," the "built" man), the assessment form (the title, the can/cannot audit), and childhood discipline ("sit still"). Each register is a system that processes a person from the outside, and the speaker has internalized all four — he administers his own doses, directs his own performance, files his own evaluation, disciplines his own brain. The absence that shouts is the clinical register proper: no diagnosis is ever named. The poem gets one word inside that perimeter — "stimming" — and places it outside the room.

### The Future Tense

"A hundred things that I'll hide from her" is the poem's most quietly devastating grammatical choice. The future tense turns concealment from a condition into a commitment — a vow being taken mid-poem, with the reader as witness. Every subsequent confession is therefore double: the reader learns the secret and simultaneously learns that she never will.

### The Unanswered Cascade

The final stanza runs three questions in escalating order — hate, love, love-of-the-wrong-man — and answers none of them. The poem ends instead on a present participle, "practicing," a verb form with no endpoint. Nothing in the poem resolves; the date is never shown beginning in earnest, the questions never close, and the rehearsal is left running as the lights go down.

## Line-by-Line Interpretation

**Lines 1–6:** The procedural opening — door, room, table — reads as confidence until lines 5–6 reclassify it as choreography. "The whole way here" quietly extends the rehearsal backwards in time: the performance did not begin at the door; it began the moment the date was agreed to. "I practice everything" is thesis, confession, and diagnosis in five words.

**Lines 7–14:** The can't-anaphora descends from household to job to conversation, "even" marking the direction. "At least not without" pivots the stanza from deficit to method — script, dosed eye contact, decades of study — and line 14 delivers the poem's central sentence: chemistry enlisted where thirty-seven years of fieldwork fell short, to "pass for" a person, with all of that verb's weight.

**Lines 15–22:** The gutter promises sex and delivers a ledger. The future tense of "I'll hide" ratifies concealment as policy. Then the triptych: each abstract possession ("My addictions," "My desires," "My emergencies") expanded by one concrete, unslidable line — the drug still in his system at the table; the chaste, self-cancelling wish to be loved as "the actual me"; the mornings that arrive as a surprise.

**Lines 23–29:** The fear inverts — love outranks hate as catastrophe — and the speaker splits into the third-person "man I built" at the table and the first-person "real me" on the sidewalk. "High, stimming, unbearable" names, once each, everything the poem has been hiding. The final line leaves the actual self in permanent rehearsal, one word from entry.

## Connection to the Collection

The poem's closest sibling is "Nobody Knows The Real Me," with which it shares its key phrase verbatim. That poem alternates received compliments ("You're so easy to talk to") with the interior rebuttal nobody hears; "Inabilities" is the mechanism behind it — the reader learns *why* he is so easy to talk to, and what the ease costs. The compliment in one poem and the script in the other are the same artifact viewed from opposite sides of the mask.

"Lovable" is the collection's other half of this poem's argument, and the two share a grammar. "Lovable" declares "I was born with several disorders" — the nearest the collection comes to naming what "Inabilities" circles — and then relocates the deficit: "He made other people *unable* to love me." "Inabilities" reverses the direction of that verdict, hauling every inability back onto the self. Read together, the two poems are a custody dispute over the same failure, and neither settlement holds: "Lovable" is too generous to the self to be believed, and "Inabilities" too merciless.

The addiction thread runs through "Hard" — which ends on its bare, capitulating list, "Trees, birds, door, couch, pills / Pills," and confesses "I'm scared of becoming an addict again" — and through "Better Off Apart," where the drugs audition for the vacant role of the love of his life. "Inabilities" assigns the substance a third job: stagehand. The drug here is neither escape nor love-substitute but production infrastructure, snorted so the performance can open on schedule.

"The mornings I woke up surprised to still be alive" joins the collection's most harrowing register, alongside "I Woke Up Low Again (August 5th)" — another morning poem, with its "bouts of wanting to survive / There's bouts of wanting to just... die" — and "Darkness In A Childhood Bedroom," where the ideation is planned rather than passive. And the collection's founding myth, "How The Little Boy Drowned," ends with a hand that grabs and lets go: "I'm not the one to help you." The uncalled-in emergencies of "Inabilities" are that poem's inheritance — a speaker who learned early that the hand lets go, and stopped reaching for one.

## Emotional Resonance

The trap at the center of "Inabilities" is structural, and the poem never flinches from its geometry: the speaker wants exactly one thing — to be loved by someone who has met the actual him — and the strategy he has adopted to make love possible is the systematic prevention of anyone ever meeting the actual him. The performance is not vanity. It is engineering, built from thirty-seven years of study and maintained at the cost of scripts, doses, and mornings he did not expect to see. And it works — that is the tragedy the last stanza faces directly. It works well enough that she might fall for it, and every success of the mask is a defeat for the man, because it proves once more that the built one is the one people can bear.

But the poem itself is the counter-move, and this is what keeps it from despair. Everything the speaker resolves to hide from her — the snorting, the stimming, the surprising mornings, the hundred things — he says *here*, on the page, plainly, in his own voice. The poem is the unmasked hello he has been out on the sidewalk practicing. The woman at the table gets the performance; the reader gets the actual him. Which means the poem does not merely describe his one desire. For as long as someone is reading it, it grants it.
