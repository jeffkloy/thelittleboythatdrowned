# "Wanderlusters (A Risqué Prayer)"

## Overview

"Wanderlusters (A Risqué Prayer)" is a prayer, and it is a real one — not a parody of the form but a scrupulously correct example of it. Every part is present and in order: the salutation, the thanksgiving, the petition, the closing through the Son, the Amen. The reverential capitals hold from the first word to the last. What the speaker puts inside that container is a request for "a fulfilling nomadic romance filled with love, care, and joy, and hot steamy sinful sex in every city You've created," and the poem's whole effect comes from the fact that nothing about the container flinches while he says it. The subtitle promises risqué, and the risqué element turns out not to be the sex. It is the theology: he names the thing he is asking for as sin, submits the request anyway, and does not ask for the category to be revised. In a collection that spends most of its pages documenting a man who cannot say what he wants to the person in front of him, this is the poem where he says all of it, in the correct form, out loud, to the largest possible addressee.

## Thematic Analysis

### The Form Is Not the Joke

It would have been easy to write this poem as a send-up, and the giveaway that it is not is the capitalization. "You," "Your," "You've," "Your Son's" — the devotional convention for referring to God, maintained without a single lapse, including in the middle of the clause about hot steamy sinful sex. A parody drops the capitals at exactly that moment, because a parody needs the reverence to collapse. Here the reverence stays switched on through the dirtiest phrase in the poem, which means the speaker is not smuggling the request past God. He is submitting it.

The structure agrees. "Dear Father God," opens; two lines of thanksgiving follow before anything is asked for; "Tonight, I come to ask You for" is petitionary diction of the most formal kind; "In Your Son's holy name I pray" routes the request through Christ as protocol requires; "Amen" closes. Nothing is skipped and nothing is shortcut. A man who wanted to be irreverent would not bother with the Son.

### "Sinful"

The single most important word in the poem is one the speaker did not have to include. He calls what he is asking for *sinful*. He does not argue that it isn't. He does not request a dispensation, plead extenuating circumstances, or reach for the modern move of insisting the appetite is natural and therefore innocent. He files the request under its accurate heading and submits it under that heading.

This is what makes the poem risqué in a way that mere content could not. Asking God for sex is a joke anyone can make. Asking God for something you have already conceded is against God — and asking sincerely, in good form, with thanksgiving first — is a genuinely awkward theological position, and the poem occupies it without blinking. The speaker's honesty costs him the easy defence. He would rather be accurate than acquitted.

### Creation as the Argument

"In every city You've created" is the line that lifts the poem out of its own joke. It is a doctrinal claim doing erotic work: the world is God's, God made the cities, and therefore a request to have sex in all of them is a request about God's own handiwork. The logic is impeccably devotional right up until you notice where it is pointed. Praise for the breadth of creation *is* the petition here; the two cannot be separated.

The title has already performed the same fusion. "Wanderlust" is one word containing two appetites, and this poem simply declines to keep them apart — the roaming and the wanting are the same drive, and the prayer asks for both at once because to this speaker they were never two things. The nomadic romance is not a setting for the sex. It is the same request stated geographically.

### The Thanksgiving Is the Real Content

Prayer convention puts thanks before petition, and the convention is usually a formality. Here it is where the poem hides its actual subject. He thanks God for two things: "the great day You've given me," and then, at more length, "for allowing me to know bravery and be vulnerable when I'm trying to find my one true love."

Read that second one against the rest of the collection and it stops being pleasant and starts being extraordinary. Bravery and vulnerability, in the presence of someone he is trying to love, are precisely the two capacities this speaker elsewhere does not have. "Inabilities" puts him on a sidewalk before a first date rehearsing his walk, running on a script written the night before and a drug taken on the way, and the man who can actually be seen never enters the room. Here, vulnerability is not a goal or a failure. It is listed as a gift already received, and thanked for in the past tense. The poem's petition is cheerful and a little outrageous; its thanksgiving is the quiet report that something has changed.

Note also that he thanks God for the *capacity to keep looking*, not for having found anyone. Nothing has worked out yet. The gratitude is for still being in the search and being able to enter it undefended.

### One True Love, Every City

The poem asks for two incompatible things and does not appear to notice. Line 3 is looking for "my one true love" — singular, destined, the language of a search with exactly one correct answer. Line 4 asks for "a fulfilling nomadic romance" distributed across "every city You've created" — plural, mobile, and by construction not singular at all. Monogamous destiny and roaming appetite, requested in consecutive breaths, with no acknowledgement that they might interfere with each other.

The poem is not confused; it is honest, and the form is what permits the honesty. A dating profile has to choose. A conversation with a person who might be the one true love definitely has to choose. Prayer is the one venue where the entire contradictory want can be put on the record without editing, because the addressee already knows both halves and there is no version of the request that would go over better. That is the argument the poem makes without stating it: this is the only place the whole thing fits.

## Literary Devices

### The Register Collision

Two vocabularies are held in one sentence: the liturgical ("I come to ask You," "In Your Son's holy name I pray") and the frankly pornographic ("hot steamy sinful sex"). "Hot steamy" is deliberately unliterary — the diction of paperback erotica and personal ads, a phrase with no poetry in it whatsoever — and it is set inside the highest formal register the language has. The collision is not decorative. It is the poem's entire claim: that both of these belong to the same man on the same night, and that neither has to be translated before being said.

### "Dear"

The salutation is a letter's, not a liturgy's. Prayers begin "Our Father," "Almighty God," "Heavenly Father"; correspondence begins "Dear." The word tilts the whole poem toward the epistolary, which matters in a collection preoccupied with messages and whether they arrive. And the addressee is named as a parent, which sets the tone for what follows: what comes next is said the way things are said to family, with the embarrassing parts left in.

### The Plural Nobody Occupies

"Wanderlusters" is a coinage, and it is plural, and no plural appears anywhere in the poem. The speaker is alone, praying in the first person singular, on a specific night. The title names a category of person — a type, a congregation — for a text that has exactly one occupant. The effect is liturgical: this reads as a prayer written to be usable, one that someone else in the same condition could pick up and say without changing a word. It is the difference between a diary entry and a form of service.

### Nothing Is Withheld

The poem's most unusual feature is structural absence: there is no turn, no undercut, no second thought. It does not end by retracting the request, apologising for it, or admitting it will not be granted. "Amen" simply closes the file. In a collection whose poems characteristically double back — the question left hanging, the consolation exposed as recitation, the confession followed by the resolution to hide it — a poem that asks for what it wants and then stops is a formal event in itself.

## Line-by-Line Interpretation

**Line 1:** "Dear Father God," — the epistolary salutation, and the paternal address. The comma is a letter's comma. The poem has established its addressee and its degree of formality before it has said anything at all.

**Lines 2–3:** The thanksgiving, and the poem's actual subject. "The great day You've given me" is small and specific rather than cosmic — he is thanking God for a day, not for a life. Then the longer gratitude: "for allowing me to know bravery and be vulnerable when I'm trying to find my one true love." Vulnerability appears here as a capacity granted, not a risk being weighed, and "when I'm trying" concedes that the search is ongoing and unresolved. He is grateful for the ability to keep going, not for an outcome.

**Line 4:** "Tonight, I come to ask You for" — bedtime, the traditional hour for prayer, and also the hour the request concerns; the word does both jobs at once. What follows escalates in a single unbroken sentence from the wholly respectable ("love, care, and joy") to the unrespectable ("hot steamy sinful sex"), and then widens to "in every city You've created," which converts the request from an appetite into a doctrine of creation. "Sinful" is the speaker classifying his own petition accurately, on the record, at the moment of asking.

**Lines 5–6:** "In Your Son's holy name I pray. / Amen." — the closing performed exactly as prescribed. The request is routed through Christ; the prayer is sealed. Nothing is taken back, softened, or qualified after the fact. The form closes over the whole of what was said.

## Connection to the Collection

This is a collection that talks to God fairly often, and never like this. "Lovable" opens "I think God messed up with everyone else" and goes on to itemise the evidence — "God also gave me a troubled mind" — before relocating the fault outward: "He made other people unable to love me." That is a grievance filed against the same addressee, in the third person, with God as the responsible party rather than the person being spoken to. "Wanderlusters" opens with thanks, in the second person, and alleges no defect at all.

"I Want To Be In Your Plans" ends on the hinge between the two postures: "If only God could give me the only thing I want, I would forever stay your man." That is a conditional and a bargain, addressed to a woman, with God appearing only as a hypothetical mechanism in the third person. "Wanderlusters" is the same impulse with the grammar corrected — no "if only," no bargain, no intermediary. He turns and asks.

The sharpest echo is in "Song": "The pills said more than I ever did in church." That line describes a man who has sat in the building and had nothing to say. "Wanderlusters" is the speech he never made — and it is worth noticing that the thing he finally says is not a confession or a plea for rescue but a want. In "Better Off Apart," prayer appears once more and belongs to someone else entirely — "someone's prayer for a safer partner," a petition he imagines being made about his ex, by a stranger, on their own behalf. He can picture the prayer. Here he is the one praying it.

The travel thread runs back to "All The Places You Will Go," which is this poem's retrospective twin. That poem catalogues cities and what each of them did to him — "I lost my head once in Amsterdam," "I learned who I was in San Francisco" — and concludes that "every city and every park would be wild" and that "Each place will ignite a different spark of yours." It is an inventory of what travel has already given him. "Wanderlusters" points the same instinct forward at the cities he has not reached yet, and asks for one specific thing in all of them.

And "the great day You've given me" quietly joins the collection's most recurrent small phrase. In "11 Moments From Love," the speaker wants to "kiss her goodbye every morning and wish her the best day at work." In "Coffee," a lover turns over to "wish you a great day." Here the great day has already been given, and the speaker's job is to thank someone for it. The same transaction appears three times in the book with three different parties in the giving role, and this is the only instance in which it has been received.

Set against "Inabilities" — the collection's other poem about wanting to be loved by someone who has met the actual you — this one is the mirror image in every respect. That poem is a man performing competence while an inventory of hidden things runs underneath. This one has no inventory, because nothing is being hidden: the addictions, the emergencies, the hundred things he'll conceal have no equivalent here. Everything the speaker wants is in the text, including the part he calls sinful.

## Emotional Resonance

The poem is funny, and the humour is not a defence mechanism, which is unusual for this book. Nothing is being deflected. It is a man in a good mood asking, in full and in the correct form, for exactly what he wants — including the embarrassing part, including the part he believes counts against him — and this is the thing the rest of the collection insists he cannot do. "Inabilities" says the actual self never makes it into the room. "Attention" asks whether the care will ever come back the other way and gets no answer. "Delivered / Read" turns on a message whose silence is the wound.

Prayer solves that problem by definition. It is address to someone who will not write back, which is the collection's most characteristic act — but it is the first time the silence is not a rejection, because a prayer never expected a reply in the first place. The speaker has found the one relationship in this book where saying everything cannot cost him anything, and what he does with it is not confess, not complain, and not ask to be fixed. He says thank you, twice, and then asks for a life full of love and joy and sin in every city on earth.

Whether it is granted is not the poem's concern, and it never raises the question. The whole want has been said, correctly, to someone. For a speaker who spends the rest of this collection unable to finish that sentence with anybody, that is not a small night's work.
