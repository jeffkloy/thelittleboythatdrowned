# "Coffee"

## Overview

"Coffee" is two lines long and does one thing completely: it concedes a small pleasure, then asks a question the concession cannot survive. The first line grants coffee its reputation and trails off. The second describes a morning — a lover turning over, a forehead kiss, a wish for the day ahead — and asks whether you have ever woken into one. Nothing is argued. The poem never claims the kiss is better than the coffee; it sets the two side by side and lets the second reveal what the first actually is, which is a thing you buy for yourself because the day has to be started somehow. And it stages the scene in the second person. The lover is *your* lover, the forehead is yours, the day being wished on is yours. The speaker stays out of the room entirely, which turns the question from a boast into an offer — or, depending on the reader, into an injury.

## Thematic Analysis

### Two Ways to Start a Day

Coffee is not chosen at random, and the poem is not really about a beverage. Coffee is the thing you take in order to become capable of the day — a stimulant, self-administered, on schedule, first thing. The forehead kiss does the same job. It arrives at the same hour, addresses the same problem, and ends with an explicit statement of purpose: *wish you a great day*. The poem places two solutions to one problem beside each other, and the difference between them is not quality but source. One you can obtain alone. The other has to be given, by someone who wanted to, while you were asleep and could not ask.

This is why the concession in the first line reads as sincere rather than sarcastic. Coffee *is* great. It works. The poem's quarrel is not with coffee's effectiveness but with what that effectiveness has been allowed to stand in for.

### The Word "Lover"

"Lover" is the erotic noun — the one word for a partner that names the sex and nothing else. Not girlfriend, not wife, not partner. And having chosen it, the poem hands that person the least erotic gesture available: a kiss on the forehead, delivered to someone unconscious.

That mismatch is the poem's engine. A lover, at that hour, could reach for you any number of ways, and this collection is elsewhere explicit about what those ways look like. This one turns over, aims for the forehead — the kiss we give children, the sick, and anyone we want to send safely into a day — and then wishes you well. The poem puts desire and caretaking in the same body, shows you only the caretaking, and trusts you to notice that the second is the one that has to be sustained daily.

### Whose Morning It Is

Notice whose lover it is. The poem says "your lover," not "mine." The speaker never describes their own bedroom, their own morning, their own partner — never claims the scene at all. They hand it to you and ask whether you have lived inside it, which converts the question from testimony into something closer to an offer. You are the one in the bed. You are asleep enough that the kiss arrives before you can arrange your face for it.

The choice also settles the poem's tone. A version that said *my lover does this* would be showing you a photograph. This one has no photograph to show. Whether the speaker has ever had such a morning is left genuinely unresolved by the text, and the poem is stronger for it: the scene may be reportage, and it may just as easily be the thing the speaker most precisely wants, built in the second person because that is the only way to hold something you do not have.

### Being Loved With the Performance Off

The gesture lands on someone asleep. That detail carries more than its casualness suggests — you cannot earn a kiss you are unconscious for, cannot perform for it, cannot arrange your face or your answer in advance. It is given to whoever you are before you are awake enough to be anyone in particular. In a collection whose speaker rehearses his walk, writes his conversations the night before, and doses out his own eye contact, the sleeping recipient is the entire point. This is love arriving while the machinery is off.

## Literary Devices

### The Concession and the Turn

The poem is built from two moves and nothing else: a concession that trails into an ellipsis, and a "But" that reopens the sentence it just closed. "And everything" is dismissive filler doing conspicuous work — it grants the point in the tone of someone who has heard it too often and is waiting for their turn. The ellipsis is the wait. By the time "But" arrives, the poem has already signalled, tonally, that the concession was a formality.

### "Have you even"

The idiom expects "ever." The poem says "even," and the substitution shifts the whole register. "Have you ever" asks about a lifetime and leaves room for a no. "Have you even" is the grammar of the comment section — *have you even read it, have you even tried* — a challenge that treats the thing in question as a floor you may have failed to clear. The poem borrows the syntax of an argument in order to ask about being kissed. It is the only aggression in the piece, and it is aimed at a reader who may have no defence against it.

### The Unanswered Question

The poem is a question, and then it stops. No answer, no second stanza, no return to coffee. The reader supplies the answer privately, and the poem's entire effect depends on which one they have: a yes turns the line into recognition, a no turns it into an itemised absence. Nothing in the text adjudicates between them. It is the same device the collection reaches for at its most exposed — the question left open because either answer is a real answer, and the speaker is not entitled to choose which one you give.

### One Clause, No Breaks

The second line runs its whole scene without a break or a comma to slow it: waking, turning, crossing to your side, the kiss, the wish. Everything happens in a single unbroken clause, at the pace of an actual morning, in which all of it takes about four seconds. The form is one held breath, which is exactly as long as the thing it describes lasts.

## Line-by-Line Interpretation

**Line 1:** "Coffee is great and everything…" — the concession is sincere about coffee and impatient with the conversation around it. "And everything" waves at all the surrounding praise without repeating any of it, and the ellipsis holds the floor rather than yielding it. The line's job is to establish that nothing is about to be attacked.

**Line 2:** "But have you even woken up to your lover turning over to your side to give you a forehead kiss and wish you a great day?" — the turn. "Even" arrives as a challenge; "your lover" hands the scene to the reader; "turning over to your side" makes the gesture cost a movement at the least willing hour of the day; "forehead" withholds the erotic kiss the noun "lover" promised; and "wish you a great day" reveals the whole thing to be aimed forward, at hours the recipient has not yet had to face. The line answers the first without mentioning coffee again.

## Connection to the Collection

"Coffee" is the receiving end of "11 Moments From Love," and the two poems share their content almost item for item. That poem's list opens with "I want to wake up next to her every morning," and its fourth entry is "I want to kiss her goodbye every morning and wish her the best day at work" — this exact scene, split in two, conjugated entirely in the giving direction. Eleven times the speaker says *I want to*, and the poem closes on "I want to be chosen, just once." "Coffee" is the same morning viewed from the other side of the bed, and the grammar carries the whole difference: in "11 Moments" the speaker performs every gesture and is chosen by no one; in "Coffee" the gesture arrives unasked, and it arrives for *you*.

The collection's mornings are otherwise uniformly bad, which is what makes this one legible. "Drone Days" opens with "Some days, depression hits me like a train the moment I wake up." "I Woke Up Low Again (August 5th)" repeats the waking twice — "Today, I woke up low again / Today, I woke up anguished again." "Inabilities" files its worst confession under the same hour: "The mornings I woke up surprised to still be alive." Waking is this speaker's most dangerous time of day, the one the rest of the book spends its energy surviving. "Coffee" is the only poem in which somebody is there when it happens.

The substance thread reaches the same finding from the other end of the shelf. This is a book of things taken to get through a day — the "Trees, birds, door, couch, pills / Pills" that "Hard" collapses into, the "Two hours of bliss" in "Drugs," the line snorted on the sidewalk in "Inabilities" so the brain will sit still. "Better Off Apart" audits its drugs by function — "The ones that make me calm, the ones that wake me up" — a job description coffee could apply for and fill legally. It is the collection's one permitted substance, the only thing on the shelf a person can admit to needing. "Coffee" starts from that sanctioned end and arrives exactly where the harder poems arrive: the chemistry works, and it is not the thing that was needed.

The second-person address is a mode this collection uses when it is being generous. "Spark and Calm" wishes the beloved "someone / who sparks your excitement / and calms your storms." "Eating Starlight" spends its length narrating a life *at* someone: "You're going to kiss your lover every day until this baby comes out" — the same noun, the same you. In each case the speaker constructs a good life in the second person and stands outside it. "Coffee" is the shortest instance of the habit and the most exposed, because the good life it builds for you is one morning long.

The reciprocity question belongs to "Attention," which asks it outright — "Will you care for me as well? / Or am I just somewhere you go to get some attention?" — before shrinking the ask to "At which point can I break down while you hold me?" "Coffee" is that question with the arithmetic already done. It does not ask whether you would give this. It asks whether anyone has given it to you.

## Emotional Resonance

The poem's power is that it cannot be answered in public. It poses a question with two available answers, and both cost something: yes, and you feel the full weight of what you have; no, and you have just been handed a precise description of an absence you may have been managing by never naming it. There is no reading that leaves the reader neutral, which is a great deal to extract from two lines.

What keeps it from cruelty is the ordinariness of what it describes. Nothing in the second line is rare, expensive, or beyond anyone. It costs a movement across a bed, four seconds, and the willingness to think about someone else's day before starting your own. That is the poem's real argument, made without ever being stated: the thing that outranks every ritual we have built for facing the morning is available to almost anyone, asks almost nothing, and is still the one most often left undone.
