# "Trying To Convince Myself That I'm Not A Bad Person"

## Overview

"Trying To Convince Myself That I'm Not A Bad Person" is a poem about leaving someone, written by a speaker whose whole mythology is built on being the one who gets left. At 3:30 one October morning he walked out on a partner, leaving her "broke and homeless in a country we both had no ties to," and the poem is the trial he convenes afterwards in his own head. The title's verb is "trying," and for most of the poem the trying is visibly hard. But the poem does reach a resolution, by changing the question. The collection's founding image, the hand that grabs a drowning boy and lets go, has been the speaker's lifelong villain. In this poem he discovers he has become that hand, and in the same movement he discovers he can no longer hate it. The last line settles the matter on its own terms. Whether he is a bad person stops being the question; what he did that morning was choose himself, and the poem stands by that choice. The abandonment and the self-rescue were the same event, seen from opposite ends of the arm, and the poem ends on the side of the rescue.

## Thematic Analysis

### The Poem He Didn't Write

The first stanza is a poem the speaker announces he is *not* writing, and then writes anyway, in quotation marks. "I'm going to make love to you every day / And I don't mean sex" — it is tender, slightly sheepish ("Of course, that'd be wonderful too, but"), and its thesis is love as service: "I'm going to embody love into all the things that I do for you." The device is a kind of paralipsis — saying the thing by declining to say it — and it does two jobs at once. It shows the reader the love that existed before the October morning, preserved as a draft. And it convicts that love of its own excess, because "all the things that I do for you" is the same ledger that comes due eight lines later as "I had already given everything to you." The love poem and the departure are written in the same currency. The draft promised to give everything; the speaker kept that promise, and the poem is about what happened when there was nothing left.

The hinge into the real poem is the title itself, quoted back at line 6: "But instead of that poem, I've been trying to convince myself that I'm not a bad person." The love poem was abandoned in favor of a defense brief. That substitution is the first abandonment the poem records, and it happens on the page before the second one is confessed.

### The Defense Brief

The second stanza is structured like testimony, and its rhetoric shows the strain of a man arguing a case he does not fully believe. It opens with the act stated plainly — "at 3:30 that October morning, I abandoned you" — and the bluntness matters: he does not say "I left," the verb a defendant would choose. He uses the prosecution's word. Then the mitigation begins, in two anaphoric pairs. "I lost my most important family member for you / I lost most of my own self-respect for you" itemizes the cost of staying. "And so I did it because I had already given everything to you / And so I did it because I had nothing left to give" offers the motive — twice, in two phrasings of the same fact. The repetition is the tell. A reason that is sufficient gets said once; a reason that has to be said twice is being rehearsed, the way someone repeats a sentence until it sounds true. This is what "trying to convince" sounds like at the level of syntax.

The stanza then turns from what he gave to what he received, in a third anaphora that escalates: "All I got from you were broken promises / All I got from you was disrespect / But most of all, all I got from you was a false sense of being cherished." The climax is not the cruelest item but the most intimate one. Broken promises and disrespect are charges anyone could level. "A false sense of being cherished" is a charge only this speaker could level, because it names the exact wound the rest of the collection is organized around — not being unloved, but being loved in a way that turns out not to have been choosing him.

### Knowing It From the Other Side

The third stanza is where the defense collapses, and it collapses through empathy rather than guilt. "People have told me that I'm too much / People have told me that I'm draining" are quoted verdicts from the speaker's own history — the collection records them almost word for word elsewhere — and they lead not to self-pity but to a terrible competence: "I know how it feels to be the one they walk away from / So I know what I did to you." The speaker cannot plead ignorance. He is the world's leading expert on what he just did.

Line 20 is a sentence fragment, and its fragmentation is the point: "My deep-seated fear of abandonment." No verb, no clause, nothing attached to it — dropped into the confession like a piece of evidence placed on a table. It is not offered as a reason. The defense brief already gave its reasons in the second stanza; this line belongs to the testimony against him. Set between "So I know what I did to you" and "I know exactly what I did to you," the fragment is the answer to what he did: he handed her the fear. The thing he has carried his whole life is now hers, delivered by the one person who knew precisely how heavy it is. The stanza closes by restating line 19 with one word added: "I know exactly what I did to you." The intensifier is the opposite of mitigation. Whatever case the second stanza was building, the third stanza has testified against it.

### The Hand

The fourth stanza is the poem's center of gravity, and it is a rereading of the collection's origin myth. "How The Little Boy Drowned" ends on a hand that grabs a drowning boy and lets go — "'I'm not the one to help you'" — and this poem quotes that voice verbatim. But it changes the tense of the title figure. Elsewhere he is "the little boy that drowned," past participle, a finished event; here he is "a little boy that drowns," present tense, a condition that keeps happening. And then the poem does what a lifetime of grievance never allowed: "I think I've despised that hand my entire life / And at 3:30 that October morning, I finally became the hand."

"Finally" is the most unsettling word in the line. It belongs to the vocabulary of arrival and relief, and it is attached here to becoming the thing he has hated most. The poem does not explain the word, and it doesn't need to — the next three lines are its explanation. "But maybe the hand was never bad / Maybe the hand was going under too / Maybe it let go because it wanted so badly to live." The triple "maybe" is a hypothesis built under pressure, because the speaker now needs it to be true. The interpretation of the hand that he could not afford as the drowning boy, he can suddenly afford as the hand. The speaker absolves the hand at the very moment he needs absolution for himself, and the poem is honest enough to let the reader see that. But the forgiveness is not a trick. It is the understanding that makes the final line possible: once letting go to survive is no longer the worst thing a person can do, he is free to do it.

### Choosing

The final stanza brings in the collection's most persistent wish — "All I ever wanted was to be chosen, just once" — and then defines what being chosen is *not*, in two lines that are really accusations: "Not to be loved out of convenience / Not to be loved out of your self-preservation." The two lines are not parallel, and the difference is one word. "Convenience" is left general, a charge that could be laid against anyone who has ever loved him. "Your self-preservation" is addressed to her, and it reaches back to the poem's own facts. When he left, she was "broke and homeless in a country we both had no ties to," which suggests that his staying was what kept her housed. Her love, the line charges, was a way of staying afloat.

Set beside the previous stanza, the line carries a strange charge. Self-preservation is exactly the motive the speaker has just granted the hand — "it let go because it wanted so badly to live." The poem now holds two drowning people with the same motive and opposite gestures: the hand that let go in order to live, and the partner who held on in order to live. He forgives the first and refuses to be loved by the second, and the distinction is the poem's working out of its own problem. Self-preservation is a legitimate reason to let go. It is not a reason to love, and love built on it is the "false sense of being cherished" of the second stanza. The final line claims the legitimate version for himself.

Then the refrain lands a third time: "So at 3:30 that October morning, I finally chose myself." The wish to be chosen just once is answered not by the partner, not by anyone in the collection, but by the speaker, and the answer is indistinguishable from the act the poem began by confessing. "Finally" returns too, now paired with the self instead of with the hand. The poem never says "I'm not a bad person," and it doesn't have to. The same minute holds both sentences — "I abandoned you" and "I chose myself" — and the poem puts the second one last on purpose. It does not erase the first; the speaker has spent three stanzas admitting it. It outweighs it. The trying in the title ends here, not with an acquittal but with a choice he is willing to stand behind. For a speaker who has spent the whole collection waiting for someone else to choose him, it is the right choice, and the poem knows it.

## Literary Devices

### The Refrain With Three Verbs

"At 3:30 that October morning" appears three times, and each time it governs a different verb: "I abandoned you," "I finally became the hand," "I finally chose myself." The timestamp never changes; only the description of the act does. The refrain functions as an instant replay viewed from three camera angles — the accused's confession, the mythic reframing, the choice — and the poem's argument is carried entirely by the change of verb. The precision of the time ("3:30," not "that night") is itself evidential: it is the kind of detail a person cannot stop knowing.

### Paralipsis

The opening stanza is a poem the speaker claims he is not writing. By quoting the abandoned love poem in full, he preserves the love he can no longer act on and lets it stand as Exhibit A — proof of what he once intended, and proof of how much "everything" he meant to give.

### Anaphora as Self-Persuasion

The poem is built almost entirely from repeated openings: "I lost … / I lost," "And so I did it because … / And so I did it because," "All I got from you … " three times, "People have told me … / People have told me," "Maybe … " three times, "Not to be loved out of … " twice. In other poems anaphora builds momentum; here it sounds like a man saying something again because it did not convince him the first time. The device enacts the title.

### Intertextual Quotation

Line 24 quotes the founding poem of the collection almost verbatim, and the poem relies on the reader recognizing it. The figure of the drowning boy, the grabbing hand, and the voice that says "I'm not the one to help you" are not introduced here; they are inherited — and then, for the first time in the collection, the speaker steps out of the boy's position and into the hand's.

### The Fragment

"My deep-seated fear of abandonment" is the only line in the poem without a verb. Isolated between two declarations of knowledge ("I know what I did to you" / "I know exactly what I did to you"), it reads like an exhibit tag: the thing itself, named, sitting exactly where the object of "what I did to you" should be. The lines on either side do the grammatical work the fragment withholds. What he did to her is this — the fear, passed from his hands to hers.

## Line-by-Line Interpretation

**Lines 1–5:** The love poem the speaker meant to write, quoted whole. Its definition of love as daily service ("all the things that I do for you") establishes the economy of giving that the rest of the poem will audit.

**Lines 6–8:** The title arrives as a line, replacing the love poem with a defense. The act is stated in the plainest possible language — "abandoned," "broke and homeless," a country with no ties — and the speaker chooses the prosecution's verb for himself.

**Lines 9–15:** The mitigation: what staying cost (a family member, his self-respect), why he left (given everything, nothing left — the same reason, stated twice), and what he received (broken promises, disrespect, and, climactically, "a false sense of being cherished"). The anaphoras are evidence of the strain behind them.

**Lines 16–21:** The defense turns on itself. Quoted verdicts from his own past ("too much," "draining") give him firsthand knowledge of being left, so he cannot claim not to know what he has done. The verbless fragment of line 20 names what he did: he gave her the fear he has carried all his life. Line 21 sharpens "I know" into "I know exactly."

**Lines 22–29:** The founding myth, retold in present tense and quoted from the source. The speaker confesses a lifelong hatred of the hand, discovers he "finally" became it, and then — through three "maybe"s — reimagines the hand as another drowning person who let go in order to survive.

**Lines 30–33:** The collection's central wish, "to be chosen, just once," narrowed by two refusals: one general ("convenience"), and one aimed at her ("your self-preservation"), which recasts her love as a way of staying afloat. The refrain returns a third time and renames the October morning as the moment he chose himself — the wish granted, by the only person in the poem left to grant it.

## Connection to the Collection

"How The Little Boy Drowned: An Introduction" is the indispensable companion. Its final lines — "A hand grabs him, and in a second, it lets go / 'I'm not the one to help you'" — supply the image this poem inhabits, and the two poems are best read as call and response: the introduction from inside the water, this poem from the end of the arm. "Closer" ends another relationship with "Once again, I am just the little boy that drowned," the speaker once more cast as the one released; "Trying To Convince Myself" is the first poem in the collection where he is the one who lets go.

"You're just too much and it's draining" is spoken to the speaker in the final section of "Conversations," headed "15 minutes before the sirens," and it ends "Please don't hurt yourself." Lines 16–17 here compress that scene to its two verdict-words, and line 18's "I know how it feels to be the one they walk away from" is the knowledge "Conversations" documents. "Therapy" puts the same verdict in a therapist's question — "Has anyone in your life told you that you're too much to handle?" — and "I Woke Up Low Again (August 5th)" carries it in quotation marks: "'too much' or 'too clingy.'"

The wish of the final stanza is nearly a quotation of "11 Moments From Love," which ends "I want to be chosen, just once. / Because I'd choose her any day." "Nobody Knows The Real Me" states it as "The only thing I want is to be loved, to be chosen." The darkest version is "Darkness In A Childhood Bedroom," which conjugates "choose" all the way through — "she chose someone else twice," "there was no her left for me to choose" — and ends "So I might choose death." Against that poem, the last line here reads as a deliberate redirection of the same verb: the choice that once pointed at death points, this time, at the self.

## Emotional Resonance

What makes this poem hard to read is that it refuses the two consolations available to it. It will not let the speaker off — he uses the word "abandoned" himself, names the conditions he left her in, and insists that he knows "exactly" what that felt like. And it will not let him drown in guilt either, because the poem's own logic will not allow it: if he condemns himself fully, he must condemn the hand fully, and the hand turns out to be a drowning person too. The speaker can only forgive himself by forgiving the thing that has hurt him most, and the poem watches him do it.

The title promises an argument, and the poem lays out the evidence honestly. The love poem is still there at the top, quoted intact, so the reader never forgets what was meant. The abandonment is there in plain words. But the poem does not leave the evidence sitting in balance. It ends on the choice, and it ends there deliberately. A speaker who has spent the whole collection waiting to be chosen finally is, by the one person who was always in a position to do it. The poem does not pretend the choice cost nothing; he knows better than anyone what it is to be left in the water. What it refuses is the old belief that staying in the water with someone is the only way to be good. Choosing himself is not the opposite of love in this poem. It is the first time he has extended love to himself, and the poem is clear that he was right to.
