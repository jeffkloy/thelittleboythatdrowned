# "Better Off Apart"

## Overview

"Better Off Apart" is a poem about emotionally surviving a love that was unstable, largely unreturned, and eventually unsafe — and about the strange fact that being right to leave does not immediately make a person feel better. In twenty-four lines the speaker tries four ways out: looking for her around every corner, looking for a replacement in chemicals, sitting alone with the loneliness the chemicals produce, and reciting the language of recovery at himself. Then, in a closing stanza, he disposes of her. The title is the poem's penultimate line rather than its last, so the reader arrives already knowing the verdict. That verdict is never in doubt. What the poem tracks instead is the distance between knowing you are better off and feeling it — and the last line is the moment the speaker closes that distance himself.

## Thematic Analysis

### The Love That Went One Direction

The poem's founding fact is that the love ran one way. She was, to him, the love of his life — the second stanza concedes as much by going out to look for "the next" one — and she did not return it, and then she left for someone else. "It's been a while since you left" names the departure without naming its cause, and the poem never supplies one, because supplying it would mean saying aloud that she found someone she wanted more. What the first stanza offers instead is an admission about himself: "it was the crazy that I once adored." "Once" is the hinge separating who she was at the start from what the thing became, and adoring someone's intensity early is not the same as agreeing to what it turned into.

The quotation marks around "love of my life" are the poem's most easily misread punctuation. They are not irony and not disbelief in the category. They mark a title — a position she genuinely held, in his account of the relationship and, as it turned out, only in his. He meant the phrase, and he meant it at the time. What wrecks the line is "next," a word conceding both that the post is vacant and that the last person to hold it was appointed unilaterally.

### Drugs as Applicants for the Position

The second stanza contains the poem's cruelest joke. "Sometimes I think I can find them in the drugs that I take" — and "them" refers back to "love of my life." The drugs are not an escape from the search; they are candidates in it, enumerated by function like a personal ad: "The ones that make me calm, the ones that wake me up." A partner who steadies you and a partner who enlivens you, and the pharmacy can fill both roles. Then the trap closes: "But all I find is the longing for love." The drugs deliver appetite, not food.

What the stanza does next is the healthiest thing in the poem. "Someone to hold, someone to care about" is the speaker applying, once again, for the role of the giver — the one-directional position that cost him everything. And then he corrects himself: "And to be held and cared about in return." That line is the poem's answer to the relationship it is about. A man who spent years loving someone who did not love him back has arrived at the ability to ask for reciprocity, and he asks for it plainly, in the flattest language available. It is not a triumphant line. It is a specification, added late, by someone who has worked out what was missing. Only then comes the qualification — "But someone who isn't you" — where the added "But" makes her the single exception carved out of an otherwise open request.

### Disordered, and Appalling

The fourth stanza's two lines are not the same sentence twice, and the difference between them is the stanza's whole argument. "You were just a disordered chapter in my life" declines to condemn. "Disordered" is clinical rather than casual — the word for a system whose order has broken, not for a person who is bad — and it arrives in the same diagnostic register as "under the influence" nine lines earlier. It describes a relationship whose central question, whether she wanted him back, stayed live the entire way through and was answered not with an answer but with her leaving for somebody else. It also breaks the metaphor carrying it, since chapters are paginated and finishable, and a disordered chapter is one whose pages will not stay in sequence.

Then everything hardens at once: "And you'll be an appalling story in someone else's now." The adjective escalates from clinical to moral, but the noun changes too, and that is the sharper move. His was a chapter — a part of a life, bounded, survivable. Theirs will be a *story*, which is both larger and, crucially, a thing that gets told. She will not merely happen to the next person; she will become the thing they recount afterwards. He withholds the moral verdict from the years he actually lived and delivers it without hesitation about a relationship he will never witness. This is not spite. It is a prediction, made by the one person qualified to make it, and the stanza's final line confirms the stakes: "someone's prayer for a safer partner" means *safer* literally. Read backwards from there, "no one could ever match your crazy" stops being a compliment about intensity and becomes something the speaker has not found a gentler word for.

### The Shoulder

"Once, you were beautiful, and I loved your shoulder" is the most devastating line in the poem, and the word is the idiom rather than the anatomy — a shoulder to lean on. Of everything he could name at the end of a love, he names the support: not her face, not her voice, but the fact that there had been someone to hold him up. This retroactively explains the rest. The drugs "that make me calm" are being asked to do the shoulder's job. "And to be held and cared about in return" is him asking, at last, for a shoulder of his own. And "someone just to hug me," screamed into an empty room, is the smallest surviving version of the same request. It also explains why "disordered" was the only adjective available to him a stanza earlier: something that held him up does not file as appalling, something that hurt him does not file as good, so he reached for the word that describes a broken arrangement rather than a bad person.

## Literary Devices

### The Title, One Line Early

Because the title announces the conclusion in advance, this is not a poem of discovery. The question of whether to leave closed before the first line and nothing reopens it. What the stanzas supply is not argument but cost. And because the title stops one line short of the ending, the poem is licensed to continue past its own conclusion — line 23 is where it was supposed to stop, and line 24 is what he said anyway.

### The Borrowed Script

Four institutional vocabularies collide here: the clinical-legal ("under the influence"), the therapeutic ("I'm in a good place," "everything that I need"), the religious ("prayer"), and finally the postal ("returning you to sender"). Each is borrowed from somewhere designed to process a person through an ending, and the escalation is the tell — by the last line he has exhausted the therapeutic language and reached for the machinery of the post office. Note precisely what the script gets wrong: it is false about his condition and true about his conclusion. He is not in a good place. He is still correct to be out.

### The Only Simile

Until the last line the poem is nearly free of figurative language, and its one metaphor — the "disordered chapter" — is a worn figure the speaker cannot make hold. Then the closing simile breaks the plain style on the way out. Both of the poem's figures are documents, things that arrive in sequence and can be refused, and the comparison collapses mid-sentence: she stops being like the mail and becomes the parcel. "Return to sender" is not a phrase but a stamp, and a returned letter is by definition unopened.

### Repetition That Shifts

The poem's engine is repetition that shifts under its own feet. "Someone to hold, someone to care about / And to be held and cared about in return / But someone who isn't you" builds through warmth into a request and lands on a disqualification. "A disordered chapter in my life / an appalling story in someone else's" changes both the adjective and the noun, so the second phrase only looks like a repetition of the first — the escalation is disguised as symmetry. Anaphora conventionally accumulates force; here it is used to move the ground.

## Line-by-Line Interpretation

**Lines 1–4:** "It's been a while" refuses to say how long, and looking for her "around every corner" describes involuntary vigilance rather than pursuit. The closing couplet — "no one could ever match your crazy… it was the crazy that I once adored" — is the founding admission, and "match" carries the language of dating as well as equivalence.

**Lines 5–11:** The quotation marks around "love of my life" mark a title rather than a doubt — she held that post, in his telling — and "next" is the word that hurts, conceding the vacancy. The stanza then runs from an audition through a failure report to the poem's one genuinely hopeful line, the request to be held in return, before closing on the single name that request excludes.

**Lines 12–16:** "Under the influence" narrates the speaker from outside himself in institutional language. "Conjure" is a magician's verb, and what the drug produces is the thing that was already there. Then "even if there were people, no one would hear me scream" removes rescue-by-presence, and the ask shrinks to a hug.

**Lines 17–21:** "I remind myself" is the tell — a reminder is what you administer repeatedly because it does not stay. The stanza moves from recited consolation to a warning issued on hard-won authority.

**Lines 22–24:** The shoulder undoes the previous stanza's filing in half a line. "We're both better off apart" then extends the finding to her as well as himself, which is more generosity than the stanza had led the reader to expect. And "I'm returning you to sender" arrives *after* the verdict, which is the last place a settled man would need to add anything.

## Connection to the Collection

The clearest sibling is "Closer," which appears to narrate the same relationship from inside it — including the fact that she "longed for someone else" while he was still there — and ends "I swear we tried, but we broke apart." This poem is its aftermath, and its title reclaims that phrase as a finding. Where "Closer" collapses into "the little boy that drowned," this one stays standing.

The unilateral title is a habit of this speaker's, and the collection knows it. "Darkness In A Childhood Bedroom" has him insisting she had "been MY ONE for an entire year," capitalised, a singularity no one else has agreed to. "Better Off Apart" is what happens after such a title lapses: the post is vacant, the appointment was never confirmed by the other party, and he is out advertising for a replacement in the second stanza of a poem about being glad she is gone.

The substance thread runs through "Drugs" — "Two hours of bliss / I can live here without you" — and "Hard," which ends on a bare list of pills. "Better Off Apart" adds something distinct: not escape but substitution, the drugs auditioning for a relationship. Its sharpest internal echo is "Spark and Calm," which wishes the beloved "someone / who sparks your excitement / and calms your storms" — precisely the two-part specification this poem hands to a pillbox. And "May Things Go Well For You" is its ungenerous twin, addressed to the same kind of departure with the opposite blessing. The reduced ask in the third stanza is "Sick" almost verbatim: "So I can feel sick / Not sick *alone*."

## Emotional Resonance

This is not a man talking himself into leaving. That decision was made before line one, and the poem never reopens it. It is a man discovering that a correct decision settles nothing on its own — that every stanza's attempt to make the rightness do some work on the feeling leaves the feeling unmoved. He is right and he is not yet better, and the poem is honest enough to hold both without letting either cancel the other.

What turns it, finally, is the last line. Returning something unopened is not avoidance; it is what a person does with contact they have decided is not safe to receive. The gesture only looks cold from outside — from inside it is competent, the correct handling of a channel that cannot be opened a little. He does not read it because reading it is how it starts again. That is the first thing in twenty-four lines the speaker does rather than endures, and it is why the poem reads as survival rather than defeat. Being better off is not the same as feeling better, and the distance between them is exactly what this poem crosses. By the last line he is on the other side of it.
